Empire of Liberty

10. A trillion USA dollars

Oslo, 1976.

I live in an old farmhouse once owned by the great mathematician Viggo Brun.

Sealed cardboard boxes line the walls. My desk is an old door, claimed at a university construction site, balanced on two sawhorses. The desktop is covered with stacks of notebooks. My memories may be gone, but my mathematical faculties are intact.

In 1919, the Viggo Brun published his greatest work, a groundbreaking proof on the nature of twin primes. Nowadays, children who apply to my Institute’s associated middle school are asked if 61 and 63 are twin primes. The answer is no, because while 61 is prime, 63 is 7 times 9. Now, how about 89 and 91? Don’t answer right away, I tell the children.

Viggo Brun, despite his genius, could never resolve the infamous Twin Prime Conjecture, which holds that the set of twin prime pairs is infinite: (5, 7), (11, 13), (17, 19), and so on. The conjecture resisted solution for hundreds of years, until I proved it in my teenage bedroom. Back then, I worked in my own mental universe. I didn’t say “prime number”; instead, I said “un-rectangle-able number,” because you can’t arrange 61 blocks into a rectangle. That’s why my teenage notebook has a proof of the “Adjacent Unrectangleable Theorem.”

The records at the local Soviet show that I purchased the Brun house with a wire transfer from an anonymous Electric Brain Cash account worth more than a trillion USA dollars, pre-inflation. If I ever knew the password to that trillion-dollar EBC account, I don’t anymore.

It could be in one of the cardboard boxes stacked up against my living room walls. The boxes were shipped here one year, five months, and thirteen days ago, according to the postmarks, around the time I started my job at the Kolmogorov Institute. I still haven’t opened the boxes. I tell myself I am busy with my proofs, but I am not that busy.
