Empire of Liberty

18. Everybody knows how it works

“I liaise between Alicent and the Ministry,” Gerald Yang said. “Mostly on Strollnet security.”

“Do you have an efficient algorithm for the discrete log problem?” I asked.

A failure to make small talk.

Gerald Yang arranged for us to meet in a different coffee shop near Alicent. He was my new boss, although still a cross-functional one, which meant I had other bosses besides him.

“We can discuss the work after you sign this non-disclosure agreement,” he said.

“I already signed an NDA to work at Alicent,” I said.

“This one covers the Ministry side.”

I read the piece of paper he handed me.

 

This work falls under confidentiality regulations.

Please provide mandatory personal identifying information.

Name: ______  ID: ________

Gender: __  Height: ____  Weight: ____

Number of recent contacts with foreign persons: __

Signatory accepts PLA commission under PLA89-901.

Authorized signature: ______

 

Where it said Weight, I wrote 5000kg.

“What does recent contacts mean?” I asked.

Gerald Yang tapped his fingers on the table.

“It doesn’t matter,” he said. “You’re required to report foreign contacts to the local police anyway. Just put whatever.”

I wrote one and signed my name. Just then, Brenda Mann walked through the door of the coffee shop. She ignored me, and I ignored her.

“You probably know that the Strollnet,” Gerald Yang was saying, “is a national security hazard cooked up in a lab somewhere and released like a virus into society. The way it works, technically, is that audio messages are chopped up, encrypted, sent through short-range hops, and re-assembled—”

“Everybody knows how it works,” I said, cutting my eyes to track Brenda. She was fishing around in her purse and backing herself into the bathroom.

“We know how to track packets through the Strollnet,” Gerald Yang was saying, “but the encryption is too strong to crack.”

“Obviously,” I said. “That’s why I asked if you solved the discrete logarithm problem.”

“According to your Alicent orientation screening,” he said, barely registering my words, “you’ve got reasonably good mathematical reasoning abilities, which is why I asked Cherry Xu for the transfer. Now, an alternative to breaking the Strollnet encryption is shutting the whole thing down.”

“Shut down the Strollnet?” I asked.

“It’s mainly used by anti-war activists to organize unsanctioned parades. You probably know that parades inevitably result in safety incidents.”

I was pretty sure most people used the Strollnet to message their friends and share music.

“The Strollnet is a distributed system,” I said. “There’s no choke point. You’d have to confiscate everyone’s portable cassette player—”

Suddenly, Brenda Mann was standing next to our table, regarding us through circular tinted glasses.

“This must be the famous Gerald Yang,” Brenda announced. “Vivian talks about you all the time.”

Gerald Yang was speechless.

“I’m Professor Brenda Mann,” she said, extending him her hand. “Professor! That sounds so pretentious. Please call me Brenda. Mind if I?”

She inhaled sharply through her nose and sat down.

“Were we meeting today for coffee?” she asked me.

“I didn’t think we were,” I answered carefully.

“Oh.”

Brenda turned to Gerald Yang.

“Vivian and I meet at a nearby café twice a week, as I’m sure you know. We collaborate on some pretty interesting problems in algorithmic number theory. I work at Tsinghua University, on leave from the University of Nova Scotia. Have you heard of it? No one has,” she laughed. Her high-speed rambling left no chance for Gerald Yang to speak. “Vivian’s a whiz. Don’t you think she’s underappreciated at the office? Vivian and I are study buddies. She’s a treasure, really. And only nineteen years old.”

Brenda paused.

“She talks about me all the time?” a puzzled Gerald Yang managed to say.

“She does,” Brenda said. “All good things. Only nineteen. Never had a boyfriend before.”

There was an awkward pause.

“Rats,” she said, looking at her watch. “I’m late for a faculty meeting. Well, I’ll leave you to it.”
