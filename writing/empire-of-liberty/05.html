Empire of Liberty

5. The handbook

Suddenly, I realized this wasn’t my struggle session.

It was Sunny Li’s.

By now, everybody knew somebody who’d been forced to leave the company and were unable to get another job in the technology sector. The tech companies exchanged a surprising amount of data: onboarding dates, graduation notices, performance evaluations, personal identifying information. Rumor had it that one graduated classmate managed to get a job at another tech company, but the job was to take care of their indoor plants, and the salary didn’t cover his rent.

Cherry Xu didn’t reply to Sunny Li’s outburst. Instead, she took out a stack of pre-filled index cards and handed them to the boy on her right.

“Should I take the card on top?” Edsger Wang asked.

“It doesn’t matter,” Cherry Xu said.

As this was happening, Sunny Li pulled out her orange, pocket-sized Alicent employee handbook and started desperately waving it around. With her finger, she tapped the cover illustration, which showed a cartoon cricket surveying the ocean from the top of a cliff.

“The Director of Corporate Identity approved this drawing!” said Sunny Li. “It’s saying—you know, implying—that the Chairman is leading our company over the edge of a cliff. I’m not spreading corporate gossip. You can see how it’s drawn for yourself, right here!”

Sunny Li started flipping the pages of the booklet, frantically.

“The handbook has a section about how corporate logos get approved—”

Struggling to find the right page, she suddenly lost momentum. I couldn’t follow exactly what Sunny Li was saying, and I thought the others might feel the same. I wanted to help her. In meetings like these, it was important to say the right thing in the right way.

“Sunny Li,” I said, “I think I get it, but could you put it in a different—”

The girl Carrie Hu cut me off, asking, “Are we talking about employee handbook section 9.16?”

Sunny Li looked up from her handbook, stricken.

“Because section 9.16 isn’t really relevant to this situation,” Carrie Hu continued.

“What?” said Sunny Li.

Krystal Zhang asked Carrie Hu to quote a more relevant section, which she did, from memory. During the Re-org, people who could quote passages from memory earned high status.

“I suggest,” said Krystal Zhang, “that we evaluate Carrie Hu’s passage in an agile, innovative way.”

Cherry Xu cleared her throat. “And let me clarify, at this point, that either Sunny Li or the Director of Corporate Identity will be graduating today, depending on the outcome of this struggle session.”
