Empire of Liberty

13. What’s an open problem?

After a few days, I forgot all about Brenda.

I did think about the number theory problem she presented, but there was an obvious blocker standing in the way of any solution, the kind of blocker that soaks up time but reveals nothing. Later, I heard that the entire Tsinghua math department became fruitlessly obsessed with the problem.

That Sunday, I went out to buy coffee so I could wake up and attack a stupider intractable equation: how to pay my rent. The cheapest coffee was a long walk from my apartment, but I had the time, and the weather was okay, according to the LED indicators on the crosswalks. For Peking people, weather is code for pollution. A popular saying in 1972 was “Pollution is the sign of a strong economy.”

The streets were packed with bicycles, and the sidewalks were filled with people. Like electrons, we walked at constant speed, spilling into the road to bypass company shuttle stations. Corporate vans ran full, even on Sundays.

I worked nine to nine, six days a week, the minimum.

Others worked more, to get ahead.

I was pulling out my Strollman headphones and got in line at the coffee shop.

“Is the coffee here any good?” came a loud voice.

I turned around and saw Brenda Mann behind me..

“Oh!” Brenda said. “I know you. You’re the girl from the lecture, aren’t you? Is the coffee here good?”

“It’s pretty bad,” I said.

Brenda said she knew a better place, if I wasn’t busy.

The place was called Monkey Hot Coffee. It was a small shop with eight mismatched tables and a giant hissing espresso machine. The light was low, because the shop’s glassfront was heavily tinted.

On the wall, a small sign announced:

“Even if you are one in a million, there are ten thousand people just like you.”

It sounded like something my mother would say.

“I was born in the year of the Monkey,” Brenda said. “How about you?”

In China, asking someone’s animal sign is indirectly asking their age, which might be considered rude. I was a Serpent, I said, making my age congruent to 7 modulo 12. If you don’t know what that is, think of an old-style analog clock that starts at midnight. The big hand is on the seven after seven hours (as only a mathematician would say), but it’s also on the seven after 19 hours. And Monkey is three years after Serpent, so Brenda’s “big hand” was still on the four, which, plus 24 hours, made her twenty-eight years old.

Math simplifies everything.

Our espressos arrived in tiny ceramic cups instead of the usual polystyrene.

“So, what are you working on?” Brenda asked.

I pulled some notebooks from my backpack, opening one to a page covered with the dwindling balances from the bank account my mother left me. Without asking for permission, Brenda flipped backwards through one notebook, then forwards through another.

Finally, she looked up at me.

“You solved all these open problems?”

“What’s an open problem?”

She sipped her espresso.

“Only nineteen years old? Your parents must be very proud you got into the math program at Tsinghua.”

“My parents are dead.”

“I’m so sorry,” Brenda said.

“Why? You haven’t done anything to be sorry about.”

Brenda gave a little laugh and said that she’d done plenty to be sorry about, but that she understood my meaning.

“Also,” I admitted, “I don’t go to Tsinghua. I got sick the day of the national college entrance exam and vomited on the steps of the testing center, so they gave me a zero.”

“What do you do for money?”

“I run out.”

“Any brothers or sisters?”

“One younger brother. He got drafted, and now he’s fighting in Vietnam.”

“You must be worried.”

“Very worried.”

Brenda leaned back with her tiny espresso cup.

“Well, you need a job. I just got here from Canada, but I have contacts in some of the Farm companies.”

The Farm was what people called the expanse of tech-company campuses that blanketed northwest Peking. Supposedly, it had been actual farmland before the economic reforms a generation ago, but no one could remember that.

Brenda poured the contents of her purse onto the café table. My eyes darted around: a tube of lipstick, a transparent bag of pills, two passports, a notebook. She plucked an orange name card out of the chaos and handed it to me.

The name on the card was Alicent Technologies.

“Strollnet that address,” Brenda said.
