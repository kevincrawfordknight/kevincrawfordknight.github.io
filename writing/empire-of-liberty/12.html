Empire of Liberty

12. Her Mandarin was flawless

Peking, 1972.

Only one other person knew that I invented Electric Brain Cash.

Brenda Mann.

What can I say about Brenda Mann?

Her Mandarin was flawless.

As she walked onto the Tsinghua University auditorium stage, I saw her light-colored hair, her large circular glasses, and her above-the-knee skirt. I guessed that was normal clothing for visiting math professors from Canada.

The auditorium was clean. Every seat-number plaque was polished. Professors occupied the first few rows in their suits and ties. Along my row, every student wore a small name badge made of matte-black plastic with engraved white characters.

Brenda Mann lectured on a problem in number theory that had baffled Western mathematicians, despite being simple to state, and her hope was that a top Chinese mathematician might crack it. Every few minutes, she posed a question to the audience and waited for a response.

Of course, we all remained respectfully silent.

But halfway through the lecture, my right arm shot up. The mortified student to my left slid down in her seat. I lowered my arm, but it was too late, and Brenda called on me.

I wasn’t a student;

I didn’t attend Tsinghua;

I’d failed the national college entrance exam;

I was an unemployed high school graduate.

I was only in the auditorium because I happened to catch a Strollnet message announcing Brenda’s lecture. I was washing my clothes at the laundromat at the time.

“Your statement is true,” I said, “for any number that’s one greater than some multiple of four.”

Brenda’s face instantly lit up into a completely disarming smile. No one in authority had ever looked at me like that.

“Yes!” she exclaimed and slapped the podium, unleashing a pile of disjointed, breathless praise that I could barely follow.

(With the benefit of hindsight, it’s clear that Brenda was high on white stimulant powder, but at the time I didn’t know that.)

After the talk, the Tsinghua Graduate Student Association hosted a reception with free food that smelled wonderful. How great, I thought, must be the life of a university student. With bad timing, I stuffed a whole steamed bun into my mouth at the moment Brenda Mann breathlessly bounded up to me.

“You know your modular arithmetic!” she said, extending her hand.

In my peripheral vision, I saw heads turn. I tried to think of what to say while I chewed my food. Brenda generously motioned me for to take my time.

“You’re not wearing an eyepatch,” I finally said. I instantly regretted my mistake. I’d conflated foreigners with pirates, probably because of an illustration in some school history book.

In my defense, I had never met a foreigner before, or a pirate.

“That’s true!” Brenda said. “Listen, I hope we can chat some more.”

She quickly handed me a card with her name and Strollnet address. I stared at it while a group male Tsinghua professors closed in and whisked her away.
