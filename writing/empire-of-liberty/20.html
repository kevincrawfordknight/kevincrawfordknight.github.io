Empire of Liberty

20. My precious

Oslo, 1976.

“Brenda Mann,” I say. “I don’t remember any Brenda Mann.”

Though a faint memory takes shape in my mind.

A woman named Brenda once gave me a book.

“This book,” says the Soviet policeman, pulling a tattered paperback from his jacket pocket.

Did this man just read my thoughts? I wondered. If he did, then I’ve just lost another memory. The worst part is, I can’t remember what I can’t remember. Sometimes I imagine myself like a frog in a pot of water over a low flame. The water is warm and comfortable. Gradually, the temperature rises halfway to the boiling point, but the frog doesn’t notice. After a while, it again reaches halfway to boiling, then halfway again, and again. The ancient Greek mathematician Zeno says that, because there is an infinity of halfway points, the water will never boil.

Maybe that’s why my mind is still functioning.

“This book,” the Soviet policeman repeats, and I take the paperback from his hand.

 

Blonde in Asia, Volume 1

by Brenda Mann

She Agreed with Their Tactics but Questioned Their Goals!

 

The cover illustration shows a sparsely-dressed woman at a bar with a small pistol strapped to her thigh. On the back cover, there’s a photo of the author. I can’t make out any facial features, though, because she wears a wide-brimmed hat and tinted glasses.

 

BLONDE IN ASIA, VOLUME 1

Chapter 1. There will always be a leading cause of death

Chapter 2. You don’t have to be Asian to work here … but it helps!

Chapter 3. Sometimes you have to set principle aside and do what’s right

 

I feel certain that I’ve never seen this book before.

Although, this is not the book Brenda gave me.

The Soviet policeman continues to question me, despite the language barrier between us.

Eventually he hands me a name card and leaves. On the back, my own physical address is written in ball-point pen, meaning: You know how to reach me, and I know how to reach you.

Once I’m sure he’s gone, I go to the cardboard boxes piled against my wall and find one labeled “Books.” With a kitchen knife, I cut the sealing tape. On top is Towering Male Figures of Mathematics (Kolmogorov Press, USSR, 1934), and just below that is a record of tidal patterns in Southern California.

At last, I find what I’m looking for.

A political fantasy novel called Master of the Ring.

The author is an elderly Englishman who escaped the Soviets and wound up in California. The plot involves two great wizards vying for control over a magical world. On the first page, inside the cover, there’s an inscription: “To V, my precious! -B.”

I turn the pages. Many passages are underlined in the same blue ink I use for mathematics. I don’t remember reading this book.

In my notebook, I update the section on my mood ring. Next to “Given by: Unknown,” I write “B.” It’s still a conjecture, not a proof.
