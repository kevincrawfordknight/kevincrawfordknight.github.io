Empire of Liberty

14. Forward to the 1970s!

Alicent offered me a job.

My interview was technical and painless, and I joined my onboarding cohort the very next day. Our first task was to memorize a poem, which rolls off my tongue even today, with my memory virtually wiped.

 

Alicent Technologies dominates world markets,

While locked in brutal competition

With business adversaries who are competent.

Forward to the 1970s!

 

Our orientation was overseen by two young “classmates,” the Alicent word for employees.

“Welcome,” said the girl. “You are lucky to join the Alicent family.”

“You have a secure job in a competitive economy,” said the boy.

“Though not all of you will succeed.”

“If you work very hard, you will succeed,” the boy assured us.

“Twenty percent of you will graduate during the first year,” said the girl.

“Fifteen percent at most,” said the boy.

“If we can’t fire, we can’t hire,” said the girl. “Isn’t that the expression?”

While she fumbled with a pack of index cards she held in her hand, the boy took over. He wrote the company’s four values on a whiteboard, the kind (he said) that automatically recognized handwritten characters and emailed everyone afterwards.

 

Accountability

Dependability

Respect

Collaboration

 

After that, we got an office tour that highlighted the Gratitude Wall, the Wall of Corporate Breakdown, and an automatic peanut grinder that made peanut butter.

Plus, lots of coffee machines.

 

A mathematician is a machine for turning coffee into theorems.

 

I’d heard that phrase somewhere.

But Alicent hadn’t hired me as a mathematician; they hired me as a FORTRAN 73 programmer. I didn’t know FORTRAN yet—they scheduled me for a training course that started two months later. Until then, I was supposed to sit at my terminal and manually verify numerical computations so that Compliance, whoever or whatever that was, could sign off on it.

Finally, I took a personality test and was assigned to Government Relations.

“A backwater,” pronounced Sunny Li, my friend from orientation.

Sunny Li seemed to possess detailed knowledge of how companies worked, despite never having worked at one. At the end of the day, I pinned a notecard to the anonymous Gratitude Wall that said, “Thanks to Sunny Li for letting me borrow a pen for the personality test.”
